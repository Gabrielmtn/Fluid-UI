Design system audit · A Small Good Thing

The control surface is eleven panels of the same six controls.

About 130 individual controls are spread across simulation, effects, kaleidoscope, display, light, presets, gestures, recording, layers, multiplayer and palettes. Almost all of them are one of seven things: a continuous value, a discrete mode, a boolean, an action, a momentary action, a stored item, or a color. What varies between them is not the job — it is the treatment. This document inventories what exists, proposes one grid and one set of parts to rebuild it on, and maps each current control onto its replacement.

The aesthetic stays where it is: a dark mixing desk. What changes is that the desk gets rack rails.

Three findings

Affordance is inconsistent where it matters most. Brush Size, Fluid and Multi-Brush are the three most-used controls on the toolbar, and all three are set as plain text with a small marker beside them. Nothing about them says "press me" until you have already pressed them. Meanwhile a checkbox two panels down gets a full box, a border and a tick.

One job, five slider treatments. The toolbar slider, the panel slider, the ramp slider, the gear-knob slider and the tiny inline slider all set a number between two bounds. They differ in track height, fill gradient, knob shape, label case, and where the value sits. A user cannot learn one and apply it.

The preset lists spend their space on nothing. Gesture cards run at roughly a third fill — an emoji, a name, a thin hint line — while the panel they sit in is the narrowest column in the app. That space is the natural home for what those cards do not currently show: a stroke preview, a bound hotkey, and the left/right split spelled out at a readable size.

2. The grid: rack units

Every control occupies one full-width row of fixed height, stacked like 19-inch gear. One control per row, no exceptions and no two-ups. The row is the only layout primitive; panels are runs of rows under a header; the whole surface is one column that scrolls.

This costs vertical space and buys three things: the label can be large, the value can be large, and every control has the same hit target and the same place to look.

4. The parts

4.1 Fader — the default value control

A mixer channel throw with a printed scale beside it. The scale is what the current sliders are missing: without ticks, a value of 0.8 tells you nothing about where 0.8 sits. Drag anywhere on the row; the whole row is the target.

0.90 0.95 1.00

Fig. 2 — Live. Drag the throw.

Migration

Replaces the panel slider and the ramp slider. Absorbs all 51 continuous values whose range is meaningful to see — Viscosity, Relaxation, Zoom, Blend, Threshold, Saturation, Cursor Size, Canvas Opacity, and every dissipation and smoothing control.

4.2 Spinner — a behaviour, not a rack row

No track at all. Grab the number, drag in any direction, and the value follows total pointer distance under pointer lock — so a long range costs no width and never hits the end of a throw.

That saving only pays somewhere narrow, which a rack row is not. Given a full-width row the spinner occupies the same height as a fader and shows strictly less — no scale, no sense of where the value sits in its range. An earlier draft of this document had it absorbing the iteration counts and Spin Speed; building it proved that wrong. In a rack row it is a slider-shaped object that is not a slider.

So it is a behaviour attached to a readout, not a row of its own, and it belongs in exactly two places: the toolbar cell, where the value is the control and there is no room for a track, and underneath the rotary in 5.1. In the panel, integer ranges of about sixteen steps or fewer take the stepper cell in 4.4; everything else takes the fader.

4.10 Momentary action with latch

Ignite fires while held. It also has a latch that keeps it on, and that latch is currently invisible — a user has no way to know the control has two modes. Give the latch a cell of its own beside the button: a 30px square holding a 9px indicator, filled when latched. The button then reads as momentary by default and sustained when the indicator is lit, and both facts are on screen at once.

Firing fills with the accent, not the heat gradient. Heat means "this much of a range is filled" and nothing else; a momentary action has no range.

4.11 Color picker

The one component in the system that floats, and the only one allowed a shadow. Everything else is flat. It is 300px wide, anchored under the well that opened it rather than centred on the screen, and its number cells are the stepper cell from 4.4 at 30px — a picker should not introduce a fourth kind of number field.

Two changes from the current picker: the corners are square, and the RGB captions sit under their fields at 10px rather than floating between them. The eyedropper keeps its cell at the left of the hue row, at the same 26px as the current-colour disc beside it.

4.12 Brush cursor

The only component that lives on the canvas, and the most efficient one in the app. A ring whose diameter is the brush size, crossed by a chord whose rotation is the brush angle and whose colour is the colour about to be painted. Three values, no panel, at the exact point the eye is already looking.

It is also the argument for the whole approach in 6.3. Nobody needs a legend to learn that scrolling changes the ring or that Shift+Scroll turns the chord, because the control and its feedback are the same object. Where a binding can be taught this way, it should be.

The only open question is whether it should also carry gate or ignite state — it is the right place for them and the wrong place for anything more.

5. Edge cases

5.1 The kaleidoscope gear knobs

The kaleidoscope rows use a toothed gear as the slider knob. It is the most characterful object in the interface and it is also the only control whose handle means something different from every other handle — it is drawn as a rotary but behaves as a linear slider. That mismatch is the problem, not the charm.

Two ways out. Retire the gear and let Kaleidoscope use faders like every other panel — cleanest, and loses the one piece of personality on the surface. Or promote it: make the gear a real endless rotary that drives the spinner behaviour from 4.2, and reserve it for the three genuinely rotational values, Angle, Twist and Spin Speed. Segments, Zoom and Blend become faders.

The second is worth the extra component. A rotary that actually rotates is not decoration, and confining it to angular quantities makes it legible rather than cute. It stays out of every other panel.

6. Explorations

6.1 The Light Shift playhead

A playhead already exists. Light Shift renders one on the colour-wheel picker with interpolated position, hue wrapping, and an inner core set to the exact colour being handed to the shader. It is well built and it is in the wrong place: it lives in a small picker inside a panel, it is read-only, and the path it travels is drawn on the canvas but represented nowhere on it.

Promote it. Keep the interpolation and the colour matching exactly as they are, and render the same head on the canvas over the drawn stroke: the path visible at low opacity, a chrome cap riding it, grabbing the cap to scrub and releasing to resume from where it was dropped. The picker head stays as the colour readout it already is. Speed stays a fader in the panel; position becomes direct.

Path Position {{ playText }}

Fig. 6 — Live. Drag the cap along the path; release to resume.

Three things fall out of this once the head is an object on the canvas rather than a dot in a picker. The path becomes editable — grab a point, move it. The head becomes a loop marker — drag a second cap on and you have an in/out range. And the same mechanism serves the recorder: a recorded layer is also a path through time, and it can be scrubbed the same way rather than through 0/0 arrows.

6.2 Compact toolbar

The horizontal strip is a rack rotated ninety degrees, and it should be built from the same parts: label above, value below, one control per cell, cells divided by the same 2px rule. Dense mode drops the track and keeps the spinner — the strip becomes a row of readouts you drag, which is what an experienced user is doing anyway.

6.3 The hotkey reminder

The reminder panel appears at the top right whenever Shift, Ctrl or Alt is held, showing a sixteen-chip default group plus whichever modifier group matches. It lands on the mixer strip, and it teaches nothing. Four things are going wrong, and only one of them is the position.

What happens Why it hurts
The default group always shows, on top of the modifier groupHold Ctrl to find Undo and you are shown sixteen bindings you did not ask for plus the three you did. The answer is 16% of the panel.
Shift alone opens itShift is a working modifier — coarse brush, rotate brush, save colour. The panel fires as noise in the middle of painting, which is exactly when the canvas must stay clear.
It is a floating layer over fixed chromeNothing reflows to make room, so it covers Multi-Brush, Time, Density and Velocity — four live readouts — at the moment a modifier is held.
The binding never appears beside its controlA user who has just dragged Brush Size with the mouse is never told that [ and ] do the same thing. Recall is supported; learning is not.

The fix is to stop treating hotkeys as a list. Bind the legend to the control instead of to the modifier: every rack row and toolbar cell carries a key cap in its right margin — the same cap the preset card uses in 4.8. Holding a modifier lights the caps of the controls that modifier reaches, in place, and dims the rest. Nothing opens, nothing moves, nothing is covered, and the binding is learned where the control lives.

Fig. 8 — Alt+Shift held. Two caps light, three cells dim, nothing is covered. The modifier badge replaces the whole floating panel.

Two bindings do not drive a visible control — Export, Mutate, Focus and the layer keys act on the app rather than on a cell. Those get a single rack row appended to the strip, at rack height, which pushes the canvas down by 34px instead of covering it. It carries only the held modifier's bindings; the default group is retired to the F1 reference, where a sixteen-item list belongs.

Ctrl ← →Cycle palette ZUndo YRedo F1 — all hotkeys

Fig. 9 — Ctrl held. Three bindings, one row, in the layout rather than over it.

Three rules make the pattern learnable rather than merely smaller. Shift alone never triggers anything — it is a live modifier during painting, and its bindings surface only on the controls they touch. The row appears after a 250ms hold, so a modifier used in passing produces no flash. And once, the first time a user changes a value by mouse that has a binding, its cap flashes for two seconds beside the control they just used — the only moment a hotkey hint has a reader's full attention.

The F1 modal stays as it is. It is the reference, it is deliberate, and nothing above replaces it.

8. Order of work

The rack row first — it is the container everything else drops into, and it is where the legibility win is. The fader second, because it is now 57 of the 130 controls — six more since the spinner gave up its rack rows. The stepper cell and segmented switch third; between them they close the affordance gap on the three most-used controls in the app. Everything after that is consolidation.

The playhead is not on that path. It is a feature, not a cleanup, and it should be built when the rest of the surface has stopped moving.
